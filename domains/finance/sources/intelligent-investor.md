## What it claims

- There is a hard line between **investing** and **speculating**: an investment operation promises safety of principal and an adequate return after analysis. Anything else is speculation. Most people speculate while believing they're investing.
- Every investor should self-classify as **defensive** (wants simplicity and safety, unwilling to put in ongoing work) or **enterprising** (willing to do genuine, continuous analytical work). The failure mode is wanting enterprising returns on defensive effort.
- **Margin of safety is the single unifying concept of sound investment.** It's the gap between the price you pay and the conservatively appraised value of the thing — that gap, not forecasting skill, is what absorbs bad luck and bad analysis.
- **Mr. Market**: imagine a business partner who every day offers to buy you out or sell you more, at wildly swinging prices driven by his own mood. His quotes are an option available to you, never an instruction. The market exists to serve you, not to inform you what your holding is worth.
- Market fluctuations are not risk in themselves. Real risk is permanent loss of capital — from overpaying, from poor business quality, or from being forced to sell at the wrong time.
- Price and value diverge routinely, and that divergence is the only source of opportunity. Popular, exciting, high-growth issues are systematically the most likely to be overpriced, because the price already assumes the growth continues.
- Asset allocation should be mechanical and bounded, not intuitive: never less than 25% nor more than 75% in stocks, with the remainder in high-grade bonds. 50/50 is the sensible default, adjusted against market extremes rather than with them.
- Concrete quality screens matter more than narrative. Graham gives seven numeric criteria for defensive stock selection rather than qualitative judgment calls.
- Most investors are badly served by their advisers, funds, and brokers, whose incentives diverge from theirs. Fees and turnover compound against you exactly as returns compound for you.
- The historical record of "growth stock" strategies is far worse than remembered, because failures get quietly delisted out of the sample.
- Graham himself repeatedly discarded and replaced his own formulas across editions as markets adapted — he treated his specific rules as perishable and his principles as durable.
- Zweig's commentary in this edition (2003) explicitly updates several thresholds for modern rates and valuations, e.g. deriving a suggested maximum P/E from prevailing high-grade bond yields rather than fixing it at 15 forever.

## What is actionable

- **Pick your lane honestly.** If you won't do continuous analytical work, run the defensive playbook (broad diversification, quality screens, fixed allocation) and stop trying to pick winners.
- **Hold allocation inside a 25–75% band.** Default 50/50 stocks/bonds. Rebalance back toward the band's centre when the market moves you outside it — mechanically, not on conviction.
- **Apply the seven defensive screens** before buying an individual stock:
  1. Adequate size (Graham's 1973 figures: ≥$100M annual sales industrial / ≥$50M assets utility — scale these for inflation, they're ~7-8x in today's money)
  2. Strong financial condition (current ratio ≥ 2:1; long-term debt ≤ working capital)
  3. Earnings stability (positive earnings every year for 10 years)
  4. Dividend record (uninterrupted for 20 years)
  5. Earnings growth (≥33% growth in per-share earnings over 10 years, using 3-year averages at each end)
  6. P/E ≤ 15 on 3-year average earnings
  7. P/B ≤ 1.5 — and the combined rule: **P/E × P/B ≤ 22.5**
- **Sanity-check the whole portfolio's earnings yield** (inverse of P/E) against the current high-grade bond yield. If stocks don't yield at least as much, you're not being paid for the risk.
- **Never let a quoted price change your view of value** unless the underlying business facts changed. Decide in advance what you think it's worth, then treat quotes as offers to accept or ignore.
- **Diversify adequately even inside a value approach** — Graham's defensive guidance is roughly 10–30 issues, not a concentrated bet.
- **Treat every number in this book as needing re-derivation for today's rates** rather than copying it literally. The method for updating the rule matters more than the rule.

## What it contradicts

Directly conflicts with `psychology-of-money.md` on **method**, while agreeing on **temperament**. This is the most useful tension in the domain so far:

- **Can you beat the market?** Graham says an enterprising investor doing real analytical work can achieve better-than-average results. Housel says most people can't and shouldn't try, and should dollar-cost average into low-cost index funds. (Partial conflict — Graham's *defensive* track is close to Housel's advice; the disagreement is whether the enterprising track is worth attempting at all.)
- **Where does edge come from?** Graham locates it in rigorous quantitative analysis of individual securities, with temperament as the enabler. Housel locates it almost entirely in behaviour, arguing finance is over-taught as physics and under-taught as psychology, and that technical skill isn't the differentiator.
- **Precise rules vs. sustainable ones.** Graham supplies hard numeric screens. Housel argues explicitly against coldly rational optimisation in favour of "reasonable" decisions you'll actually stick with. Notably, `psychology-of-money.md` predicted this exact conflict and cited Graham walking back his own formulas late in life — which this book's own edition history confirms.
- **What a portfolio should look like.** Graham: a screened set of quality issues bought below appraised value. Housel: accept that a few tail winners drive everything and most picks disappoint, which points toward broad ownership rather than selection.

**Where they agree, strongly:** margin of safety / room for error as the central survival concept (Graham coined it, Housel devotes a chapter to it); avoiding ruin matters more than maximising returns; forecasting is unreliable; the investor's own behaviour under stress is the main risk to manage.

## Open questions

- The book gives no rule for updating its own rules. Zweig patches some thresholds for 2003 conditions, but there's no systematic method for re-deriving the screens in a 2026 rate and valuation regime.
- The defensive screens exclude most of the modern index by construction — asset-light software businesses fail the P/B test almost by definition. The book never resolves whether that's the screen protecting you or the screen being obsolete in a market where value sits in intangibles.
- Entirely US large-cap equities and bonds, from a specific interest-rate era. Says nothing about international/emerging markets, currency exposure, or a non-US tax context.
- Silent on crypto and private markets by construction (predates both) — relevant to the `trading` domain, which needs its own sources rather than stretching this one.
- It's about managing a securities portfolio, not about running a business's cash. Doesn't transfer directly to operating decisions in `y-interval` or `b2b-whales`.
- Dividend-record criterion (20 unbroken years) is far more restrictive now that buybacks have substantially replaced dividends as the payout mechanism; the book doesn't anticipate that substitution.
